\title{The Null Space of the Reality Gap:\\
An Exactly Computable Equivalence Class in Simulator Parameter Space}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Amitosh Mishra}
\IEEEauthorblockA{\textit{Independent Researcher}\\
amitosh.mishra81@gmail.com}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We show that the set of simulator errors a learned controller cannot perceive
is, for a broad class of driven mechanisms, an exactly computable Lie group of
parameter scalings---and that knowing it changes what one should identify and
what one should randomise. On a triple inverted pendulum we first observe that
model-error \emph{direction} dominates magnitude: at identical Euclidean
displacement in link-mass space, one direction leaves swing-up success at
$100\%$ while another destroys it, and in a 90-cell landscape mass-ratio
distortion explains $97.6\%$ of transfer variance while overall scale explains
$0.7\%$. We then derive, rather than guess, the full scaling symmetry group of
the closed loop by solving a linear system on scaling exponents. The group is
three-dimensional and contains a generator that scales every mass, inertia,
damping and friction coefficient \emph{together with the controller's loop gain
and force limit}; verified numerically, a $1000\times$ error in all of them
changes the trajectory by $7\times10^{-12}$\,rad. Experiments establish
that no further null direction survives a random search (24 directions at equal
magnitude all fail, including one $79\%$ aligned with the group), that it is
\emph{policy-independent} (drop spread $0.4$ points across seeds, in a system
where seed alone moves transfer by 77 points), that it \emph{generalises} to
other robot classes and changes with the action interface. Finally, an
accidental defect---a CAD export in which no materials had been assigned---
produced a real model error whose free and fatal components the theory
separated correctly before measurement. We also report five
pre-registered hypotheses that failed, including our own attempts to turn the
geometry into a predictive scalar, which conventional trajectory fidelity still
beats. \textbf{All validation is in simulation}: the physical machine is
specified but not assembled, so what we demonstrate is a model-to-model
equivalence with a proved structure, not a measured sim-to-real transfer. We
mark that boundary explicitly throughout. Code, pre-registrations and raw
results: \repo.
\end{abstract}

sim-to-real transfer, reinforcement learning, system identification, symmetry,
domain randomisation, underactuated systems

\section{Introduction}

A simulator is judged by how accurately it reproduces a robot. That framing
presumes every physical parameter deserves attention in proportion to how wrong
it is. On the system studied here that presumption is false in an unusually
sharp way: there exists a set of parameter errors, of unbounded magnitude, that
a learned controller provably cannot perceive, and that set can be written down
in closed form before any experiment is run.

The plant (\S\ref{sec:setup}) is a triple inverted
pendulum performing swing-up. Scaling every mass, inertia, damping and
friction coefficient in it by
$1000$---together with the velocity loop's gain and its force limit---leaves the
closed-loop trajectory unchanged to $7\times10^{-12}$\,rad. Scaling a single
link mass by $1.5$ destroys the task. The first error is larger by every norm
(Fig.~\ref{fig:task}).

\subsection*{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A derived symmetry group.} We solve for \emph{all} scaling
symmetries of a driven $N$-link chain by reducing invariance to a linear system
on scaling exponents (\S\ref{sec:group}). The group is three-dimensional for
$N=1..4$ and contains a generator coupling plant parameters to controller
parameters---a coupling no dimensional analysis of the plant alone produces.
\item \textbf{It is the complete \emph{continuous scaling} null space}, and we
find no evidence of any further null direction (\S\ref{sec:t1}): of 24 random
directions applied at a magnitude known to be fatal, none is null, and one that
is $79\%$ aligned with the group still scores $0.0\%$. We are careful to
distinguish the proved statement from the searched one.
\item \textbf{It is policy-independent} (\S\ref{sec:t2}), which is what makes it
usable: it can be computed offline from equations, with no trained policy. In
the same system, random seed alone moves transfer by 77 points.
\item \textbf{A prospective validation on an accidental defect}
(\S\ref{sec:cad}), and \textbf{five pre-registered failures}
(\S\ref{sec:fail}), including our own attempts to make the geometry predictive.
\end{enumerate}

Every hypothesis in this paper was registered in the public repository, with
thresholds and kill conditions, in a commit preceding its data. We report the
rejections in the same detail as the confirmations.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Domain randomisation} covers reality by training across a parameter
distribution \cite{tobin2017,peng2018,openai2020}. Widths are chosen
per-parameter and isotropically. An exact symmetry implies that budget spent
inside it buys nothing, because the sampled plants are one plant in different
coordinates; quantifying that is left to the re-measurement noted in the
source.

\textbf{Control-oriented identification} argues that identification should
preserve closed-loop relevant properties rather than open-loop accuracy
\cite{gevers2005,hjalmarsson2009}. We reach a stronger conclusion on a narrower
class: certain parameter combinations are \emph{exactly} unobservable to the
task, so no precision on them is required at all.

\textbf{Dimensional analysis and dynamic similarity} give scaling relations
from units alone. Our group is not obtainable that way: its principal generator
includes a controller gain $k_v$ and a saturation limit $F_{\rm clamp}$, which
are properties of the closed loop rather than of the mechanism.

\textbf{Bisimulation metrics} \cite{ferns2004,castro2020} formalise behavioural
equivalence by quotienting the \emph{state} space. The object here is an
equivalence on \emph{physical parameters}, induced by a task and an actuation
interface.

\section{System and Protocol}\label{sec:setup}

A triple inverted pendulum on a belt-driven cart, modelled from CAD of a
physical build. The policy commands the cart at $250\,$Hz through a velocity
loop, $F = \mathrm{clip}(k_v(v_{\rm cmd}-\dot x), \pm F_{\rm clamp})$, with
$k_v = 400\,$N\,s/m and $F_{\rm clamp} = 349.5\,$N; a first-order actuator lag
of $100\,$ms and a $4.0\,$m/s speed limit are always present. The upright
equilibrium has $\lambda_{\max} = 15.5\,$rad/s.

PPO (\texttt{rsl-rl}) in Isaac Lab: $[256,256,128]$ MLPs, ELU, $\gamma=0.999$,
$\lambda=0.95$, clip $0.2$, lr $3\!\times\!10^{-4}$, entropy $0.006$, 4096
environments, 1000 iterations, frozen across every experiment. An episode
succeeds when it never terminates early, the tip reaches upright, and the tip
is \emph{still} upright over the final quarter. Reward is never used as a
success metric: one run reached reward 58 while never lifting the tip.

\textbf{The experimental unit is the policy seed.} This matters more than usual
here: \S\ref{sec:fail} reports a 77-point transfer spread across seeds at
otherwise identical settings.

\textbf{Two assets.} Results before \S\ref{sec:cad} use the original CAD
extraction. \S\ref{sec:cad} shows that extraction assigned no materials, and
every result after it uses the corrected asset with a policy retrained on it.
We state which is used throughout.

\section{Direction Dominates Magnitude}\label{sec:aniso}

To ask whether direction matters, magnitude must be held fixed. For uniform
scaling by $c$ the displacement in link-mass space is $(c-1)m_0$; each
transverse condition places that same norm into one link. Every condition at a
given $c$ is then exactly equidistant from nominal (verified: spread
$0.0$).

Table~\ref{tab:matched} gives the matched-magnitude sweep. The anisotropy
$A = P(\text{uniform}) - \overline{P(\text{transverse})}$ is
$+100.0$ points at $c=2.5$, seed spread $2.2$. A stronger version removes the
matching question entirely: uniform $\times 8$ displaces $1.53\,$kg and scores
$100.0\%$, while link~1 $\times 8$ displaces $1.24\,$kg---smaller in $L_1$,
$L_2$ and $L_\infty$---and scores $0.0\%$.

Sweeping the two axes separately, on the \emph{corrected} asset with

\[
\text{ratio distortion } \delta: 97.6\%, \qquad
\text{overall scale } c: 0.7\%.
\]
Across a $5\times$ range (Fig.~\ref{fig:landscape}), overall scale is not
merely less important than ratio distortion: it is not a variable. Drive saturation never exceeds $0.007\%$ of
any cell, so this is not an authority artefact.

\section{The Symmetry Group, Derived}\label{sec:group}

\subsection{Method}
Write the equations of motion with a symbol per coordinate and per time
derivative. Assign every parameter, coordinate and time a scaling exponent,
$p \to \lambda^{k_p}p$, $t\to\lambda^{k_t}t$, so a derivative of order $r$
carries $\lambda^{k_{\rm coord}-rk_t}$. Invariance requires every additive term
of an equation to acquire the same power of $\lambda$; each pair of terms gives
one linear constraint, and the null space of the constraint matrix is the Lie
algebra of the scaling group. This is Buckingham-Pi applied to the equations
rather than to the units, and unlike guessing it returns \emph{completeness}.

\subsection{Result}
For the $3$-link chain on a velocity-loop-driven cart with viscous and Coulomb
dissipation, at fixed control rate, the group is three-dimensional (820
constraints). Its principal generator is
\begin{equation}
\big(m_i,\,I_i,\,b_{\rm joint},\,f_{c,\rm joint},\,b_{\rm cart},\,
m_{\rm cart},\,k_v\big)\;\longmapsto\;c\,(\cdot).
\label{eq:gen}
\end{equation}
The same solver returns dimension $3$ for $N = 1,2,3,4$, and independently
discovers that $L_3$ carries no dynamics---correct, since link~3 is the free
end and only $l_{c,3}$ enters---and recovers the known uniform-inertial
symmetry under a prescribed base. Both are validations we did not supply.

\begin{proposition}
Let a planar $N$-link chain hang from a cart driven by
$F=\mathrm{clip}(k_v(v_{\rm cmd}-\dot x),\pm F_{\rm clamp})$, with viscous and
Coulomb dissipation. Then \eqref{eq:gen}, extended by $F_{\rm clamp}\to
cF_{\rm clamp}$, leaves the closed-loop trajectory exactly invariant for any
$c>0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$T$ and $V$ are linear in $\{m_i,I_i,m_{\rm cart}\}$ and the dissipative
generalised forces are linear in $\{b,f_c,b_{\rm cart}\}$, so every term of the
unclamped equations is homogeneous of degree one in these parameters. For the
drive, $\mathrm{clip}(c\,k_v e,\pm cF_{\rm clamp}) = c\,\mathrm{clip}(k_ve,\pm
F_{\rm clamp})$ because clipping commutes with positive scaling. Every term
therefore scales by $c$; the equations are multiplied by $c$ and it divides
out.
\end{proof}

The proof is elementary. What is not elementary, and is what the solver
supplies, is that these are \emph{all} the scaling symmetries, and that
$k_v$ and $F_{\rm clamp}$---a controller gain and a saturation limit---belong
to a symmetry of the mechanics.

\subsection{Numerical verification}
Integrating the closed loop outside the simulator, with the same policy and the
same commanded velocities, the maximum link-angle deviation under
\eqref{eq:gen} is $\sim\!5\times10^{-12}$\,rad at $c = 2, 10, 100$ and $1000$.
Every leave-one-out control fails by radians: dropping the dissipation gives
$26.5$, dropping $k_v$ gives $15.9$, dropping the cart mass $3.7$, dropping the
links $20.3$, dropping the clamp $15.6$. All five components are necessary and
together they are exactly sufficient.

\subsection{Realisability is gated by authority}
In Isaac, with a policy scoring $100.0\%$ at $c=1$ on the corrected asset, the
generator holds at $c=16$ ($100.0\%$), $c=32$ ($99.6\%$) and $c=64$
($100.0\%$)---a $6400\%$ error in every inertial and dissipative parameter.
Without scaling $F_{\rm clamp}$, the same generator gives $0.0\%$: preserving
the equations does not preserve the force demand, and a symmetry that asks for
more force than the drive can deliver is not realisable. This is the same
boundary that gates the inertial symmetry alone, arriving from the theory
rather than from measurement.

\section{No Null Directions Outside the Group}\label{sec:t1}

Completeness in \S\ref{sec:group} concerns \emph{scalings}. It does not exclude
other behaviourally null directions. We tested 24 random unit directions in the
$8$-dimensional log-parameter block $[m_{1..3},I_{1..3},m_{\rm cart},k_v]$, each
at the magnitude of a $32\times$ scaling, each with $F_{\rm clamp}$ scaled in
proportion to its $k_v$ component so that authority is never the reason a
direction fails.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lr}
\toprule
control, $c=1$ & $99.6\%$\\
\textbf{group direction} & $\mathbf{100.0\%}$\\
24 random directions & $0.0$--$0.8\%$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/nullspace.png}
\caption{Every direction is applied at the same magnitude, with the force limit
scaled per direction so authority is never the reason one fails. Alignment with
the group does not help: the null space is the group, not a basin around it.}
\label{fig:null}
\end{figure}

None is null (Fig.~\ref{fig:null}). A direction $79\%$ aligned with the group
scores $0.0\%$: the null set is not a basin around the group, and partial
alignment buys nothing.

\textbf{What this does and does not establish.} \S\ref{sec:group} \emph{proves}
that the group is the complete set of \emph{scaling} symmetries---that is a
null-space computation, not a sample. This section is a search, and a search of
24 directions cannot establish that a continuous 8-dimensional parameter space
contains no other behaviourally null set. A nonlinear curve, an isolated
equivalence, a discrete transformation or a state-dependent one could all evade
24 random directions. The honest statement is therefore:
\[
G \;=\; \text{complete continuous \emph{scaling} null space (proved)},
\]
\[
\text{no further null direction found under random search (evidence)}.
\]
We keep these separate throughout.

\section{The Group is Policy-Independent}\label{sec:t2}

Almost everything measurable in this system depends violently on the policy
(\S\ref{sec:fail}). The symmetry does not. Two independently trained policies,
both $100.0\%$ at $c=1$:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
policy & $c=1$ & $c=16$ & $c=64$\\
\midrule
\texttt{CORR\_s1} & $100.0\%$ & $100.0\%$ & $100.0\%$\\
\texttt{CORR\_s2} & $100.0\%$ & $99.6\%$ & $99.6\%$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The spread of the drop across seeds is $0.4$ points. This is what makes the
group useful in practice: it is a property of (plant, interface), computable
offline from equations, with no policy in hand.

\section{It Generalises, and the Interface Changes It}\label{sec:t6}

The solver needs only a Lagrangian and a control law. Applied unchanged:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
system & principal generator\\
\midrule
cart-pole, velocity loop & $m_{\rm pole},m_{\rm cart},I_{\rm pole},k_v$\\
cart-pole, force command & $m_{\rm pole},m_{\rm cart},I_{\rm pole},u$\\
2-link arm + payload, PD & $m_1,m_2,m_{\rm pay},I_1,I_2,k_{p},k_{d}$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Every system admits a generator coupling all inertial parameters to the
controller's gains, and the group \emph{changes with the action interface}. The
arm case is a robotics statement on its own: payload-mass uncertainty is
absorbable by gain scaling.

\section{Prospective Test: An Accidental Defect}\label{sec:cad}

Midway through this work we discovered that the CAD export underlying the
simulator had no materials assigned: $122$ of $130$ bodies carried
SolidWorks' default $1000\,$kg/m$^3$, including \emph{all} 16 bodies composing
the three links. Steel shafts, bearings and collars were $7.9\times$ too light;
PETG arms printed at $25\%$ infill were $\sim\!2.1\times$ too heavy. Correcting
both changes the link masses by $[0.81, 1.06, 0.48]$ and shifts $l_{\rm com}$ by
$+53.6\%$ on link~1.

This is a real model error, of the commonest possible kind, and it arose
independently of the theory. It is nonetheless a \emph{model-to-model}
discrepancy: the corrected asset is our best estimate of the machine, not the
machine. What this section tests is whether the theory correctly decomposes an
error it did not design, not whether it predicts hardware. Decomposed, it is
\[
\text{overall scale } c = 0.744,\qquad
\text{ratio distortion } 2.21\times .
\]
By magnitude it is dominated by a $26\%$ mass reduction; by the geometry it is
almost pure ratio distortion. The landscape of \S\ref{sec:aniso}, measured
before the defect was known, therefore predicted that the scale part would be
free and the ratio part fatal. Measured, with the frozen policy:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lr}
\toprule
scale component alone & $100.0\%$\\
ratio component alone & $1.2\%$\\
the real defect & $0.8\%$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Removing the scale component from the real defect changes the outcome by $0.4$
points. Policies retrained on the corrected asset recover $100.0\%$ on two of
three seeds.

\section{What the Geometry Does Not Do}\label{sec:fail}

Five pre-registered hypotheses failed, and they bound the claim.

\textbf{It does not yield a predictive scalar.} We twice tried to rank the
38-condition heterogeneous suite by a geometry-derived distance: once with a
fitted metric tensor ($\rho = -0.322$) and once with the exact quotient
distance to the symmetry orbit ($\rho = -0.243$). Conventional short-window
trajectory RMSE beats both at $\rho = -0.532$. The geometry says exactly what
is \emph{free}; it does not rank what is not. One detail survives: the
component of each condition \emph{along} the generator is uncorrelated with
success ($\rho = +0.065$, $p=0.76$), the theorem appearing unprompted in data
collected long before it was derived.

\textbf{The interface does not reorder behaviour.} We pre-registered that
replacing velocity command with force command would change the measured
anisotropy by $\geq 40$ points. It changed it by $4.3$ ($100.0$ vs $95.7$). The
interface changes the group \emph{structurally} (\S\ref{sec:t6}) without
changing which errors are behaviourally fatal at the operating point tested.

\textbf{Geometry does not change what RL learns, in the way we predicted.}
Training in an equivalence-direction simulator transferred \emph{worse} than
training in a transverse one ($1.0\%$ vs $4.9\%$), opposite to prediction.

\textbf{In-simulator performance is uninformative, and worse.} Across 15
policies selected the only way a practitioner can---best measured success in
their own training simulator---own-simulator success spans $2.0$ points while
transfer spans $100.0$; $\rho = -0.090$ ($p=0.75$). Among the 12 policies
strictly between $2\%$ and $98\%$, the correlation is $\rho = -0.570$:
selecting the nominally best policy is mildly counterproductive. Within one
simulator, random seed alone moves transfer by $77.0$ points while moving
in-simulator success by $0.8$.

\textbf{Fidelity cannot certify.} Two conditions $1.51\%$ apart in trajectory
RMSE---below what the measure resolves across initial conditions---transfer at
$0.0\%$ and $100.0\%$.

\section{The Hardware Experiment, Specified in Advance}\label{sec:hw}

The machine is not built, so we specify the physical test now rather than after
seeing its outcome. Two configurations are manufactured to be \emph{equally
wrong} in parameter space, one along the group and one transverse to it:
\[
\Delta\theta_\parallel \in T_\theta G,
\qquad
\Delta\theta_\perp \in (T_\theta G)^\perp,
\qquad
\|\Delta\theta_\parallel\| \;\gg\; \|\Delta\theta_\perp\| .
\]
The parallel case is realised by adding mass to every link \emph{and} the
carriage while scaling the drive's velocity-loop gain and current limit by the
same factor---all four are quantities we can set. The transverse case adds a
much smaller mass to a single link. The \emph{same frozen policy} is deployed
in both, with matched initial conditions and repeated trials. The theory
predicts
\[
P_{\rm success}(\parallel) \;\gg\; P_{\rm success}(\perp)
\]
despite the parallel configuration being the larger error by every norm. A
failure of that ordering falsifies the paper's central claim on hardware, and
the prediction is registered in the repository before the robot exists.

\section{Limitations}

\textbf{No hardware.} The machine is specified and partly procured but not
assembled; \S\ref{sec:hw} states the physical test in advance so it cannot be
fitted afterwards. Every number here is simulation and $R^\star$ is a stand-in
for reality, so the strongest claim available to us is a model-to-model one
with a proved structure.

\textbf{The proposition is elementary.} Its content is a homogeneity argument.
The non-obvious parts are completeness, the inclusion of controller parameters,
and the empirical realisability boundary---not the scaling itself.

\textbf{Other robot classes are symbolic only.} \S\ref{sec:t6} solves for their
groups; it does not train policies on them.

\textbf{Material densities are estimated, not weighed.} PETG at $25\%$ infill is
bracketed $400$--$570\,$kg/m$^3$. The \S\ref{sec:cad} conclusion is unchanged
across that bracket, but no link has been on a scale.

\section{Conclusion}

The errors a learned controller cannot perceive form a group, the group can be
computed in closed form from the equations and the control law, it is the
complete set of scaling symmetries with no further null direction found, it
does not depend on the
policy. Whether that translates into a measurable saving in randomisation
budget is the subject of an experiment currently being re-run. What it does not provide is a scalar that ranks the errors outside it;
for that, conventional trajectory fidelity remains the better tool, and the two
are complementary rather than competing.
