\title{Direction, Not Magnitude: Anisotropy of the\\Sim-to-Real Gap in Learned Control}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Amitosh Mishra}
\IEEEauthorblockA{\textit{Independent Researcher}\\
amitosh.mishra81@gmail.com}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
A simulator in which every link mass is wrong by $700\%$ trains a swing-up
policy that transfers perfectly. A simulator in which a \emph{single} link mass
is wrong by the same factor---a strictly smaller error in every norm---trains a
policy that fails completely. We study this reversal on a triple inverted
pendulum on a cart and show that it is not an accident of one perturbation.
Holding the Euclidean distance of the model error fixed in link-mass space and
varying only its \emph{direction} moves swing-up success by 100 percentage
points. Part of the effect has an exact explanation: for a planar $N$-link chain
driven through a kinematically prescribed base, uniform scaling of all link
inertias multiplies the passive Euler--Lagrange equations by a constant and
leaves the trajectory invariant. We prove this symbolically for $N\!=\!1..4$ and
give its counterexamples. We then pre-registered and tested the natural
generalisation---that the equivalence class is a property of the \emph{action
interface}, since the proof needs the base to be prescribed---and it
\textbf{failed}: replacing cart-velocity command with direct cart-force command
changes the measured anisotropy by $4.3$ points against a pre-registered
threshold of $40$. Two further pre-registered scalar metrics were also rejected.
What survives is sharper and less comfortable: the anisotropy is real, large and
robust to the interface; short-horizon trajectory fidelity is the best aggregate
predictor of transfer yet admits simulator pairs $1.5\%$ apart in fidelity with
a 100-point transfer gap; and in-simulator success is uncorrelated with transfer
($\rho=-0.09$, $n=15$), with seed alone moving transfer by 77 points at
identical settings. All experiments, pre-registrations, failures and analysis
code are public at \repo.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
sim-to-real transfer, reinforcement learning, underactuated systems, system
identification, domain randomisation
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}

Practitioners choose where to spend effort on a simulator by asking how large
each modelling error is. Mass is uncertain to $10\%$, so randomise mass by
$10\%$; the actuator model is crude, so identify it better. This implicitly
treats the space of model errors as isotropic, with a single scalar---error
magnitude, or a fidelity score---ordering how dangerous a discrepancy is.

On the system studied here that ordering is wrong, and not marginally. Scaling
every link mass by $8\times$ displaces the model by $\|\Delta m\| = 1.53\,$kg
and leaves swing-up success at $100.0\%$. Scaling one link by $8\times$
displaces it by $1.24\,$kg---smaller in $L_1$, $L_2$ and $L_\infty$---and gives
$0.0\%$. The larger error is free; the smaller one is fatal.

\subsection*{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A matched-magnitude experiment} isolating direction from
magnitude in model-error space, showing a 100-point transfer gap between
directions at identical $\|\Delta m\|$ (\S\ref{sec:aniso}).
\item \textbf{An exact symmetry} explaining the privileged direction for planar
$N$-link chains under prescribed base motion, proved symbolically with its
hypotheses and counterexamples (\S\ref{sec:sym}).
\item \textbf{A pre-registered test of the interface hypothesis that failed}
(\S\ref{sec:iface}), reported as the pre-registration specified. Two further
pre-registered fidelity metrics were also rejected (\S\ref{sec:fidelity}).
\item \textbf{Evidence that in-simulator performance is uninformative about
transfer} (\S\ref{sec:blind}), including a 77-point transfer spread driven by
random seed alone at otherwise identical settings, together with a separate
pre-registered test of whether the geometry changes what RL \emph{learns}
(\S\ref{sec:h3}), which also failed.
\end{enumerate}

Every hypothesis in this paper was registered in the public repository, with
thresholds and kill conditions, in a commit that precedes the data. Two of five
were rejected. We regard the rejections as part of the result.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Domain randomisation} trains across a distribution of simulator
parameters so that reality is covered by the training support
\cite{tobin2017,peng2018,openai2020}. Randomisation widths are typically chosen
per-parameter and isotropically; our results suggest a large fraction of that
budget can be spent along directions the closed loop cannot resolve.

\textbf{Control-oriented system identification} argues that identification
should preserve closed-loop relevant properties rather than open-loop prediction
accuracy \cite{gevers2005,hjalmarsson2009}. We arrive at a related conclusion
from the opposite direction: rather than weighting an identification criterion,
we exhibit parameter directions that are \emph{exactly} unobservable to the task
under a stated actuation hypothesis.

\textbf{Actuator modelling for transfer} has repeatedly been identified as
decisive, with learned actuator models a standard remedy \cite{hwangbo2019}. Our
\S\ref{sec:iface} was designed to test whether the action interface determines
which physical errors matter. It did not support that hypothesis at the
pre-registered operating point.

\textbf{Behavioural equivalence} in RL, via bisimulation metrics
\cite{ferns2004,castro2020}, formalises when two states or MDPs are
interchangeable for control. That work quotients the \emph{state} space; the
object here is an equivalence on \emph{physical parameters} induced by a task
and an actuation interface.

\section{System, Task and Protocol}\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Plant}
A triple inverted pendulum on a belt-driven cart, modelled from CAD of a
physical build. Link masses are $m_0 = [0.178, 0.100, 0.077]\,$kg with
pivot-to-pivot lengths $[0.250, 0.250, 0.316]\,$m; the translating cart mass is
$0.79\,$kg. The drive is a $400\,$W servo through a $40$-tooth GT2 pulley
(pitch radius $12.73\,$mm), giving a rated cart force of $99.7\,$N, a peak of
$349.5\,$N, and a rated cart speed of $4.0\,$m/s. The upright equilibrium has
$\lambda_{\max} = 15.5\,$rad/s, i.e.\ a $64\,$ms divergence time constant.

\subsection{Task, action interfaces and success}
Episodes begin at dead hang. The policy commands the cart at $250\,$Hz through
one of two interfaces:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{velocity} --- commanded cart velocity through a stiff inner loop,
$F = \mathrm{clip}(k_v(v_{\mathrm{cmd}} - \dot x), \pm F_{\mathrm{clamp}})$
with $k_v = 400\,$N\,s/m. This is what the STEP/DIR hardware accepts.
\item \textbf{force} --- commanded cart force directly, with the inner loop
removed ($k_v = 0$).
\end{itemize}

Both carry an identical first-order actuator lag ($\tau = 100\,$ms), the same
$349.5\,$N clamp and the same $4.0\,$m/s speed limit, and the force action is
scaled to the \emph{peak} force so it is never given less authority than the
velocity loop could demand. An episode \textbf{succeeds} when it never
terminates early, the tip reaches upright, and the tip is \emph{still} upright
over the final quarter of the episode. Reward is never used as a success
metric: two runs reached high reward while never lifting the tip.

\subsection{Learning and evaluation}
PPO (\texttt{rsl-rl}) in Isaac Lab, $4096$ environments, $1000$ iterations,
frozen hyperparameters. Checkpoints are selected by measured success in the
policy's \emph{own} training simulator---the only criterion available without
access to reality. Evaluation uses $256$ independent episodes.

\textbf{The experimental unit is the policy seed, not the episode.} This is not
a formality: \S\ref{sec:blind} measures a 77-point transfer spread across seeds
at identical settings, which exceeds most effects one might wish to claim.

\section{The Gap is Anisotropic}\label{sec:aniso}

\subsection{Separating direction from magnitude}
To ask whether \emph{direction} matters, magnitude must be held fixed. For
uniform scaling by $c$ the displacement in link-mass space is
$\Delta m = (c-1)m_0$, of norm $(c-1)\|m_0\|$. Each transverse condition places
that same norm entirely into one link,
\begin{equation}
\text{scale}_i \;=\; 1 + \frac{(c-1)\|m_0\|}{m_{0,i}},
\label{eq:matched}
\end{equation}
so every condition at a given $c$ is exactly equidistant from nominal by
\eqref{eq:matched}
(verified: spread $0.00\mathrm{e}{+}00$ across conditions). Without this the
experiment would merely rediscover that larger errors are worse.

Matching by norm invites one objection: at $c = 2.5$ it compares uniform
$\times 2.50$ against link-3 $\times 5.22$, because absorbing the whole
displacement into the lightest link takes a large factor. We therefore also
report a \textbf{dominated} scheme: uniform $\times c$ against a \emph{single}
link $\times c$. There the uniform perturbation is strictly larger in $L_1$,
$L_2$, $L_\infty$ and in fractional terms on every link that moves, so it is
handicapped rather than flattered.

Table~\ref{tab:matched} gives the velocity-interface sweep, three seeds, all
clearing a $95\%$ nominal-competence gate.

The anisotropy, defined as
$A = P(\text{uniform}) - \overline{P(\text{transverse})}$ at matched
$\|\Delta m\|$, is $+100.0$ points at $c = 2.5$ with a per-seed spread of
$2.2$ points. Under the dominated scheme it is $+99.7$: the \emph{larger} error
transfers better, which no choice of magnitude matching can produce on its own.

Uniform scaling at $c = 8$ shows $0.0\%$ early termination, so the drive still
holds the system; the sweep is inside the authority envelope. Separate
experiments place the authority boundary between $c = 10$ and $c = 20$.

\section{An Exact Inertial Symmetry}\label{sec:sym}

\begin{proposition}
Let a mechanical system have coordinates $(x,q)$ where $x$ is kinematically
prescribed and $q$ is passive, carrying no generalised force. The kinetic and
potential energies of the passive bodies,
\begin{align}
T &= \textstyle\sum_i \tfrac12 m_i \|v_i(q,\dot q,x,\dot x)\|^2
     + \tfrac12 I_i \omega_i^2,\\
V &= \textstyle\sum_i m_i g\, h_i(q,x),
\end{align}
are linear in $\{m_i, I_i\}$, so scaling all of them by $c>0$ gives
$\mathcal L \to c\mathcal L$. The passive Euler--Lagrange equations are
homogeneous, $\frac{d}{dt}\partial_{\dot q}\mathcal L - \partial_q \mathcal L
= 0$, hence become $c$ times themselves and $c$ divides out. Therefore
$q(t;c) = q(t;1)$ for the same $x(t)$ and initial conditions.
\end{proposition}

The result is exact, holds for any $N$, and does not involve the base mass at
all: once $x$ is prescribed the base leaves the passive equations. We verify it
symbolically for $N = 1..4$ by checking
$\mathrm{simplify}\big(e|_{m\to cm, I\to cI} - c\,e\big) = 0$ for every passive
equation $e$.

\textbf{The hypotheses are the interesting part.} The same check
\emph{fails}---as it must---when (i) only a subset of links is scaled, breaking
uniformity; (ii) a non-scaling generalised force such as fixed joint friction
acts on $q$; or (iii) $x$ is not truly prescribed, which on real hardware means
the drive has finite authority. We confirm (iii) causally elsewhere: with frozen
policy weights, uniform $\times 20$ gives $0.0\%$ at $k_v\!=\!400$/$349\,$N and
$100.0\%$ at $k_v\!=\!4000$/$7000\,$N, and neither raising the gain alone nor the
clamp alone suffices.

\section{Is the Equivalence Interface-Conditioned?}\label{sec:iface}

\subsection{The hypothesis, and why it is natural}
The proof needs $x$ prescribed---a statement about the \emph{actuator}, not the
mechanics. A stiff velocity loop with authority to spare prescribes the base; a
force command does not. Analytically the consequence is stark. At matched
$\|\Delta m\| = 0.109\,$kg, the worst-case relative change in link acceleration
over 400 states is

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
direction & velocity & force\\
\midrule
uniform $[c,c,c]$      & $1.4\mathrm{e}{-}14$ & $7.5\mathrm{e}{-}02$\\
transverse $[c,1,1]$   & $1.2\mathrm{e}{-}01$ & $1.3\mathrm{e}{-}01$\\
transverse $[1,1,c]$   & $2.0\mathrm{e}{-}01$ & $2.0\mathrm{e}{-}01$\\
\midrule
ratio worst/best       & $1.4\mathrm{e}{+}13$ & $2.6$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

In the equations, direction is everything under one interface and nearly
irrelevant under the other. We pre-registered the prediction that this survives
into learned closed-loop control: $A_{\text{vel}} - A_{\text{force}} \geq 40$
points, with $A_{\text{force}} \geq A_{\text{vel}}$ as a kill condition, three
seeds per arm, everything but the commanded variable held identical.

\subsection{Result: the prediction failed}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
arm & $A$ per seed & mean & \\
\midrule
velocity & $100.0,\,100.0,\,100.0$ & $+100.0$ & \\
force    & $100.0,\,96.9,\,90.2$   & $+95.7$  & \\
\midrule
\multicolumn{2}{l}{gap} & $+4.3$ & (threshold $40$)\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

All six policies cleared the competence gate. The kill condition was
\emph{not} met---the effect points the predicted way---but $+4.3$ points is an
order of magnitude below threshold, so the hypothesis is unsupported at the
pre-registered operating point.

\textbf{Where the inference went wrong.} The analytical table is correct; the
error was inferring a small behavioural gap from a small ratio. Swing-up success
is a threshold, not a linear readout of acceleration error. Under force command
uniform scaling still does roughly half the damage of a matched transverse
error, and half is enough to sit on the far side of the threshold. A
$1.4\mathrm{e}{+}13$ ratio and a $2.6$ ratio produce the same 100-point gap.

\subsection{Post-hoc: the effect exists at larger perturbations}
Fig.~\ref{fig:aniso} shows why the pre-registered probe was blind. Along the
equivalence direction the velocity arm is flat to $\|\Delta m\| = 1.53\,$kg,
while the force arm decays: $99.0\%$, $95.7\%$, $61.5\%$, $3.5\%$. The
interface effect is real and large---but it lives beyond $c^\star = 2.5$, which
was fixed in advance precisely to avoid saturation. We report the
pre-registered verdict as the result and this as an observation requiring
independent confirmation.

\section{Fidelity is Informative but Cannot Certify}\label{sec:fidelity}

Across 38 heterogeneous conditions spanning actuation, dissipation, rigid-body
and combined errors, conventional short-window trajectory RMSE is the best
aggregate predictor of transfer ($\rho = -0.506$). Two pre-registered
policy-conditioned alternatives were \textbf{rejected}: a stability-weighted gap
$D_{SW}$ ($\rho = -0.415$) and a task-margin projected risk $R_{TC}$
($\rho = -0.406$), the latter failing all four of its frozen kill criteria.

But the winner cannot \emph{certify}. With a tolerance frozen in advance
($10\%$ relative, $\geq 50$-point gap, plausible conditions only), a
second-order actuator condition and a joint-damping condition differ by $1.51\%$
in trajectory RMSE and transfer at $0.0\%$ and $100.0\%$. Twenty-eight
admissible pairs fall within tolerance. Trajectory fidelity is therefore
\emph{informative statistically and ambiguous locally}---a sharper claim than
``insufficient'', and one our own data supports rather than contradicts.

\section{The Simulator Cannot Tell You Which Policy to Ship}\label{sec:blind}

Fifteen policies were trained across six simulators and deployed into a common
target $R^\star$ fixed before any of them existed. Each was selected on measured
success in its own training simulator.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
 & mean & range & spread\\
\midrule
own training simulator & $99.7\%$ & $98.0$--$100.0$ & $2.0$\\
deployment into $R^\star$ & $27.1\%$ & $0.0$--$100.0$ & $100.0$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Spearman $\rho = -0.090$ (permutation $p = 0.749$, $n = 15$). The selection
criterion every practitioner has available is uninformative about the quantity
every practitioner wants.

The controlled version removes the simulator entirely. Within the nominal arm,
where architecture, reward, protocol and iteration count are identical and only
the random seed differs, own-simulator success spans $0.8$ points while transfer
spans $\mathbf{77.0}$ points ($100.0\%$, $71.1\%$, $23.0\%$).

Nor is the criterion stable within a run. Comparing each run's best checkpoint
to its final one, both in the same simulator, the mean drop is $38.6$ points
($99.7\% \to 61.0\%$); $11$ of $15$ runs lose more than $20$ points and only
$3$ end at their best. The default practice---train for a fixed budget, take the
final policy---would report $61.0\%$ where selection reports $99.7\%$. These
policies are not converged; they are samples from an oscillating process.

\section{Does the Geometry Change What RL Learns?}\label{sec:h3}

The results above concern one frozen policy across many simulators. The
sim-to-real question is $S_i \to \pi_i \to R^\star$. We pre-registered that
training in an equivalence-direction simulator (uniform $\times 8$) would
transfer at least 20 points better than training in a transverse one
(mass $[1.5,1,1]$).

\textbf{It failed, in the opposite direction}: $1.0\%$ versus $4.9\%$. A
plausible account---at $c\!=\!8$ the force demand is $\sim\!8\times$ nominal, so
\emph{training} occurs in a saturated regime and the policy fits
saturation---is post-hoc and untested; the authority-matched arm that would test
it has not been run. A companion prediction was supported: two simulators
indistinguishable by trajectory fidelity trained policies differing by $64.6$
points. Equivalence for a \emph{fixed} policy is evidently not equivalence for
\emph{training}.

\section{Limitations}

\textbf{Everything here is simulation.} The hardware described in
\S\ref{sec:setup} is specified and partly procured but not assembled. No claim
in this paper has touched a real robot, and the honest status of the
sim-to-real framing is that $R^\star$ is a stand-in for reality, not reality.

\textbf{One plant, one task.} The exact symmetry is proved for general planar
$N$-link chains under prescribed base motion, but every measurement is on one
triple pendulum performing swing-up. Whether other systems admit
task-conditioned equivalence directions is untested.

\textbf{Three seeds against a 77-point nuisance spread.} Effects smaller than
$\sim\!40$ points are not resolvable at this seed count. The anisotropy
($+100$) clears it comfortably; the interface gap ($+4.3$) does not, and we do
not claim a small true effect has been ruled out.

\textbf{Policies are not converged}, as \S\ref{sec:blind} documents. Results
therefore describe a selected snapshot of an oscillating training process, which
is standard practice and is also part of what \S\ref{sec:blind} criticises.

\section{Conclusion}

Model-error magnitude does not order sim-to-real transfer. On this system,
moving $1.53\,$kg along one direction in link-mass space is free while moving
$1.24\,$kg along another is fatal, and an exact Lagrangian symmetry explains
part of why. The natural generalisation---that the action interface selects
which errors matter---is intuitive, analytically supported by thirteen orders of
magnitude, and \emph{not} what learned closed-loop behaviour does at the
operating point we pre-registered.

The practical reading is uncomfortable. A practitioner cannot rank simulators by
a scalar fidelity score, cannot rank policies by in-simulator success, and
cannot assume a large parameter error is more dangerous than a small one. What
is left is the geometry itself, which is measurable, large, and---on this
evidence---more robust than the theory we advanced to explain it.
