\title{Raw Physical Parameters Are the Wrong Coordinates\\
for Sim-to-Real Uncertainty}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Amitosh Mishra}
\IEEEauthorblockA{\textit{Independent Researcher}\\
amitosh.mishra81@gmail.com}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Domain randomisation and system identification for sim-to-real transfer
usually represent model uncertainty as a distribution over raw simulator
parameters: masses, inertias, damping coefficients, gains. We give evidence
that these are the wrong coordinates. For a learned swing-up controller on a
triple inverted pendulum, model errors of identical Euclidean size in link-mass
space differ by 100 percentage points in success depending only on their
direction, and a uniform error of $1.53$\,kg is harmless while a $1.24$\,kg
error confined to one link is fatal. Over a 90-cell landscape, mass-ratio
distortion explains $97.6\%$ of the variance in success and overall scale
$0.7\%$. The redundant directions are not new: we verify that they are exactly
the dimensional-similarity classes of Buckingham-$\Pi$, which include
controller gains and actuator limits and which dimensionless-policy methods
already exploit to transfer controllers between similar systems. What that line
of work leaves open, and what this evidence motivates, is using the similarity
quotient as the coordinate system in which simulator uncertainty is
\emph{represented, randomised and identified}. We report a naturally occurring
test of that view: a CAD export in which no materials had been assigned
produced a real model error of $26\%$ in overall scale and $2.21\times$ in mass
ratio, and a landscape measured before the defect was discovered correctly
predicted that the scale component would cost nothing ($100.0\%$ success) and
the ratio component would destroy the task ($1.2\%$; the full defect
$0.8\%$). We also report five pre-registered hypotheses that failed, including
attempts to turn this geometry into a scalar predictor of transfer, which
conventional trajectory fidelity still outperforms. \textbf{All validation is
in simulation}: the machine is not yet assembled, and the hardware test is
specified in advance. Code, pre-registrations and raw results: \repo.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Sim-to-real pipelines describe what they do not know about a robot in the
simulator's own parameters. Domain randomisation samples masses, inertias and
friction coefficients from per-parameter ranges \cite{tobin2017,peng2018,
openai2020}; system identification estimates the same quantities from data.
Both implicitly treat a large error in any of them as more dangerous than a
small one.

For the learned controller studied here that ordering is not merely
imprecise; it is reversed along identifiable directions (Fig.~\ref{fig:task}).
Some very large parameter errors are provably invisible to the closed loop,
while much smaller errors of a different kind destroy it. The invisible
directions are the classical similarity classes of dimensional analysis. What
is not classical is the consequence we draw from them: \emph{if a family of
parameter vectors describes one plant, uncertainty over that family is not
uncertainty at all, and should not be represented, sampled, or estimated as if
it were.}

The plant is a triple inverted pendulum on a velocity-controlled cart,
performing swing-up with a PPO policy (\S\ref{sec:setup}).

\subsection*{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Evidence that raw-parameter distance misorders transfer risk}
for a learned controller (\S\ref{sec:aniso}): a 100-point success gap between
directions at identical Euclidean distance, and a landscape in which ratio
distortion explains $97.6\%$ of variance and overall scale $0.7\%$.
\item \textbf{Identification of the redundant directions with the
dimensional-similarity quotient} (\S\ref{sec:quot}). We check, rather than
assume, that the null generators of the closed-loop equations are exactly the
mass- and length-unit scalings plus a parameter that never enters; that
actuator limits must be included in the class for it to be physically
realisable; and that a random search finds no other null direction.
\item \textbf{A prospective decomposition of an accidental real modelling
defect} (\S\ref{sec:cad}), predicted by a landscape measured before the defect
was known.
\item \textbf{Five pre-registered negative results} that bound the claim
(\S\ref{sec:fail}).
\item \textbf{A pre-specified hardware and identification protocol}
(\S\ref{sec:hw}) that tests the representational claim, rather than similarity
transfer, which prior work has already demonstrated on a physical pendulum
\cite{pascoa2025}.
\end{enumerate}

Every hypothesis was registered in the public repository, with thresholds and
kill conditions, in a commit preceding its data.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Domain randomisation} trains across a distribution over simulator
parameters so that reality lies inside the training support
\cite{tobin2017,peng2018,openai2020}. The distribution is specified in raw
physical coordinates, and choosing it well is widely recognised as the hard
part of the method.

\textbf{Dimensional analysis in control and learning.} Girard showed that
control policies restated in dimensionless variables transfer exactly between
dimensionally similar systems and involve fewer parameters \cite{girard2024}.
Pascoa \emph{et al.} use Buckingham-$\Pi$ scaling of observations and actions
for zero-shot RL policy transfer, including on a physical pendulum, assuming
the target system's context parameters are known \cite{pascoa2025}. Charvet
\emph{et al.} define dimensionless MDPs whose policies are equivariant to
context changes, train in a single environment, and name the need to measure
the perturbation variables as the approach's main weakness
\cite{charvet2025}. Kir~Hromatko \emph{et al.} transfer dimensionless MPC
controllers across scales \cite{hromatko2025}. None of these, to our knowledge,
uses the similarity quotient as the space in which simulator uncertainty is
randomised or identified; a systematic review should precede any claim of
priority.

\textbf{Identifiability.} Classical robot identification recovers only base
inertial parameters, the linear combinations to which the inverse-dynamics
regressor is sensitive \cite{gautier1990}, and recent work characterises
observability geometrically \cite{wensing2024}. Control-oriented identification
argues for fidelity in closed-loop-relevant properties
\cite{gevers2005,hjalmarsson2009}. The similarity quotient is related but
distinct: it identifies plants that are behaviourally indistinguishable under a
fixed control law and actuator limits, and it includes gains and limits that no
inertial regressor contains.

\textbf{Behavioural equivalence} in RL, via bisimulation metrics
\cite{ferns2004,castro2020}, quotients the \emph{state} space; the quotient
here is on physical parameters.

\section{System and Protocol}\label{sec:setup}

A triple inverted pendulum on a belt-driven cart, modelled from CAD of a
physical build. The policy commands the cart at $250\,$Hz through a velocity
loop, $F = \mathrm{clip}(k_v(v_{\rm cmd}-\dot x), \pm F_{\rm clamp})$, with
$k_v = 400\,$N\,s/m and $F_{\rm clamp} = 349.5\,$N; a first-order actuator lag
of $100\,$ms and a $4.0\,$m/s speed limit are always present. The upright
equilibrium has $\lambda_{\max} = 15.5\,$rad/s.

PPO (\texttt{rsl-rl}) in Isaac Lab: $[256,256,128]$ MLPs, ELU, $\gamma=0.999$,
$\lambda=0.95$, clip $0.2$, lr $3\!\times\!10^{-4}$, entropy $0.006$, 4096
environments, 1000 iterations, frozen across every experiment. An episode
succeeds when it never terminates early, the tip reaches upright, and the tip
is \emph{still} upright over the final quarter. Reward is never used as a
success metric: one run reached reward 58 while never lifting the tip.

\textbf{The experimental unit is the policy seed.} \S\ref{sec:fail} reports a
77-point transfer spread across seeds at otherwise identical settings.

\textbf{Two assets.} Results in \S\ref{sec:aniso} use the original CAD
extraction for the matched-magnitude sweep and the corrected asset for the
landscape; \S\ref{sec:cad} explains the difference. We state which is used
throughout.

\section{Raw Distance Misorders Transfer Risk}\label{sec:aniso}

To ask whether direction matters, magnitude must be held fixed. For uniform
scaling by $c$ the displacement in link-mass space is $(c-1)m_0$; each
transverse condition places that same norm into a single link. Every condition
at a given $c$ is then exactly equidistant from nominal.

Table~\ref{tab:matched} gives the sweep. The anisotropy
$A = P(\text{uniform}) - \overline{P(\text{transverse})}$ is $+100.0$ points at
$c=2.5$, with a seed spread of $2.2$. Removing the matching question entirely,
uniform $\times 8$ displaces $1.53\,$kg and scores $100.0\%$, while link~1
$\times 8$ displaces $1.24\,$kg---smaller in $L_1$, $L_2$ and $L_\infty$---and
scores $0.0\%$.

On the corrected asset, sweeping $m = m_0\cdot[c(1+\delta),c,c]$ over
$6\times5$ cells and two seeds, a two-way decomposition gives
\[
\text{ratio distortion } \delta: 97.6\%, \qquad
\text{overall scale } c: 0.7\%.
\]
Across a $5\times$ range of overall scale (Fig.~\ref{fig:landscape}), success
barely moves. Drive saturation never exceeds $0.007\%$ of any cell, so this is
not an actuator-authority artefact.

\section{The Redundant Directions Are Dynamic Similarity}\label{sec:quot}

\subsection{What the null directions are}
We solved for every exponent scaling $p\to\lambda^{k_p}p$ of parameters,
coordinates and time that leaves the closed-loop equations of motion
invariant, by requiring every additive term of each equation to acquire the
same power of $\lambda$. At the fixed control rate the policy runs at, the
solution space is three-dimensional for chains of $N=1$ to $4$ links. We then
checked each basis element against a table of physical dimensions. The three
generators are exactly (i) a change of mass unit, (ii) a change of length unit
together with gravity, and (iii) $L_3$, the length of the free end link, which
never enters the equations; freeing time adds (iv) a change of time unit.
Nothing in the group goes beyond Buckingham-$\Pi$. The solver is a convenience,
not a result \cite{girard2024}.

The mass-unit generator is the one relevant to modelling error:
\begin{equation}
\big(m_i,\,I_i,\,b_{\rm joint},\,f_{c,\rm joint},\,b_{\rm cart},\,
m_{\rm cart},\,k_v,\,F_{\rm clamp}\big)\;\longmapsto\;c\,(\cdot),
\label{eq:gen}
\end{equation}
every quantity with mass dimension one. The corresponding invariance is
elementary: the unclamped equations are homogeneous of degree one in these
quantities, and $\mathrm{clip}(c\,k_v e,\pm cF_{\rm clamp}) =
c\,\mathrm{clip}(k_v e,\pm F_{\rm clamp})$. Integrated outside the simulator
with the same policy and commands, trajectories under \eqref{eq:gen} agree to
$\sim\!10^{-11}$\,rad for $c$ up to $1000$, and removing any single component
from the scaling breaks agreement by radians.

\subsection{Why this matters for uncertainty representation}
Two consequences are practical rather than mathematical.

\textbf{The class includes the controller.} A randomisation or identification
scheme over raw parameters that varies masses while holding gains and limits
fixed leaves the similarity class, even when every mass is scaled together. In
Isaac, with a policy scoring $100.0\%$ at $c=1$ on the corrected asset,
\eqref{eq:gen} holds at $c=16$ ($100.0\%$), $c=32$ ($99.6\%$) and $c=64$
($100.0\%$), but the same scaling \emph{without} $F_{\rm clamp}$ gives
$0.0\%$. Force demand scales with the plant, and a similar plant the actuator
cannot drive is not similar in practice.

\textbf{Nothing else was found to be free.} We sampled 24 random unit
directions in the 8-dimensional log-parameter block
$[m_{1..3},I_{1..3},m_{\rm cart},k_v]$ at the magnitude of a $32\times$ scaling,
scaling $F_{\rm clamp}$ with each direction's $k_v$ component so that
authority is never the reason one fails.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lr}
\toprule
control, $c=1$ & $99.6\%$\\
similarity direction & $100.0\%$\\
24 random directions & $0.0$--$0.8\%$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/nullspace.png}
\caption{Every direction is applied at the same magnitude, with the force limit
scaled per direction. Partial alignment with the similarity direction does not

A direction $79\%$ aligned with the similarity direction still scores $0.0\%$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:null}). This is a search, not a proof: 24 directions cannot
exclude a nonlinear, discrete or state-dependent equivalence elsewhere in a
continuous parameter space. What is proved is that the \emph{scaling} null
space is exactly dimensional similarity; what is observed is that nothing else
was free. Invariance under \eqref{eq:gen} is also, as expected,
policy-independent: two independently trained policies lose at most $0.4$
points at $c=64$.

\section{A Real Defect, Decomposed in Advance}\label{sec:cad}

Midway through this work we found that the CAD export underlying the simulator
had no materials assigned: $122$ of $130$ bodies carried SolidWorks' default
$1000\,$kg/m$^3$, including \emph{all} 16 bodies composing the three links.
Steel shafts, bearings and collars were $7.9\times$ too light; PETG arms printed
at $25\%$ infill were $\sim\!2.1\times$ too heavy. Correcting both changes the
link masses by $[0.81, 1.06, 0.48]$ and shifts $l_{\rm com}$ by $+53.6\%$ on
link~1.

This is the commonest kind of simulator construction error, and it arose
independently of the analysis. It is a \emph{model-to-model} discrepancy---the
corrected asset is our best estimate of the machine, not the machine---so what
it tests is whether an uncertainty representation decomposes an error it did
not design. By magnitude the defect is dominated by overall scale; in similarity
coordinates it is almost pure ratio distortion:
\[
\text{overall scale } c = 0.744,\qquad
\text{ratio distortion } 2.21\times .
\]
The landscape of \S\ref{sec:aniso}, measured before the defect was known,
therefore predicted the scale component to be free and the ratio component
fatal. Measured, with the frozen policy:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lr}
\toprule
scale component alone & $100.0\%$\\
ratio component alone & $1.2\%$\\
the real defect & $0.8\%$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Removing the scale component from the real defect changes the outcome by $0.4$
points. A raw-parameter error budget would have ranked this defect as a modest
$26\%$ mass error. Policies retrained on the corrected asset recover $100.0\%$
on two of three seeds.

\section{What Did Not Work}\label{sec:fail}

Five pre-registered hypotheses failed, and they bound the claim.

\textbf{The geometry yields no predictive scalar.} Ranking a 38-condition
heterogeneous suite by a geometry-derived distance---once with a fitted metric
tensor ($\rho = -0.322$) and once with exact distance to the similarity orbit
($\rho = -0.243$)---loses to conventional short-window trajectory RMSE
($\rho = -0.532$). Similarity coordinates say which errors are free; they do not
rank the ones that are not, and trajectory fidelity remains the better tool for
that. The component of each condition \emph{along} the similarity direction is
uncorrelated with success ($\rho = +0.065$, $p=0.76$), as the analysis
predicts.

\textbf{The action interface does not reorder behaviour.} Replacing velocity
command with force command was pre-registered to change the anisotropy by
$\geq 40$ points; it changed it by $4.3$ ($100.0$ vs $95.7$).

\textbf{Training in a similar simulator did not help as predicted.} Training at
uniform $\times 8$ transferred \emph{worse} than training at a transverse error
($1.0\%$ vs $4.9\%$), opposite to prediction.

\textbf{In-simulator success is uninformative, and worse.} Across 15 policies
selected by best success in their own training simulator, that success spans
$2.0$ points while transfer spans $100.0$; $\rho = -0.090$ ($p=0.75$). Among the
12 policies strictly between $2\%$ and $98\%$, $\rho = -0.570$. Within one
simulator, seed alone moves transfer by $77.0$ points while moving in-simulator
success by $0.8$.

\textbf{Fidelity cannot certify.} Two conditions $1.51\%$ apart in trajectory
RMSE transfer at $0.0\%$ and $100.0\%$.

\section{Hardware Protocol, Specified in Advance}\label{sec:hw}

Similarity transfer on a physical pendulum has already been demonstrated
\cite{pascoa2025}; repeating it on this machine would not test the paper's
claim. The hardware protocol instead tests the \emph{representation}, and is
registered before the robot exists.

\textbf{Test 1: decomposition of a real mismatch.} After weighing every link and
measuring the drive, a physical configuration change is applied and decomposed
into similarity and ratio components using the pre-registered landscape. The
predicted success ordering is committed before deployment. The change set
includes a large similarity-preserving modification---added mass on every link
and the carriage, with the velocity-loop gain and current limit scaled
accordingly---and a much smaller ratio-breaking one, with the same frozen
policy, matched initial conditions and repeated trials.

\textbf{Test 2: identification in similarity coordinates.} From a fixed budget
of hardware trajectories, fit (i) every raw parameter and (ii) only the
dimensionless coordinates. Train a policy on each identified model and deploy
both frozen. The representational claim predicts that (ii) reaches equal or
higher success from fewer trajectories. A result the other way falsifies it.

\section{Limitations}

\textbf{No hardware.} Every number here is simulation; the strongest available
claim is model-to-model.

\textbf{The representational claim is motivated, not yet demonstrated.}
\S\ref{sec:aniso} and \S\ref{sec:cad} show that raw coordinates misrank risk
and that similarity coordinates decompose a real error correctly. Whether
randomising or identifying in those coordinates yields a measurable saving has
not been shown: the randomisation experiment is being re-run after an
invalidating bug, and \S\ref{sec:hw} is unexecuted.

\textbf{The similarity analysis is classical.} Its content is Buckingham-$\Pi$;
the search in \S\ref{sec:quot} is evidence, not a proof, that nothing else is
free.

\textbf{One robot class.} No second system has been tested experimentally.

\textbf{Densities are estimated, not weighed.} PETG at $25\%$ infill is
bracketed $400$--$570\,$kg/m$^3$; the \S\ref{sec:cad} conclusion holds across
that bracket.

\section{Conclusion}

For a learned controller on an underactuated robot, the size of a simulator
error in raw physical parameters is a poor guide to its consequence. A large
part of raw parameter space is redundant by dynamic similarity, that
redundancy includes the controller's gains and limits, and a real modelling
defect that looked like a modest mass error was correctly identified in
similarity coordinates as almost pure ratio distortion. The implication we
draw, and have not yet fully tested, is that simulator uncertainty should be
represented in the similarity quotient rather than in the raw parameters that
happen to define the simulator.
